Detailed Reviews

WD My Passport 5TB

The WD My Passport 5TB is the last bastion of the portable hard disk drive, and it serves a specific audience extremely well. Physically, it's a compact slab of plastic that weighs next to nothing, making it easy to toss into a laptop bag. The key spec here is 5 TB of storage for just under $100—that's roughly 2.5 cents per gigabyte, which is unbeatable if you need to archive years of photos, music libraries, or completed projects you rarely touch. I tested it by transferring a 200 GB folder of raw video files over USB 3.0, and it took around 45 minutes, which is typical for a 5400 RPM HDD. That's not a drive you want to edit video from, but for nightly backups or cold storage, the speed is perfectly adequate. The included WD Backup software is genuinely useful: it can schedule automatic backups of your Documents, Pictures, and Desktop folders, and the hardware encryption (256-bit AES) means that if you lose the drive, your data is protected without any software overhead. I did notice that the drive vibrates slightly when spinning, and the included USB cable is short, so you'll need to keep it close to your computer. The biggest drawback is that this is a spinning disk—if you drop it while it's reading, you risk damaging the platters. For anyone on a tight budget who needs maximum capacity for archival purposes, this is the smart buy.

Samsung includes software-based AES 256-bit encryption, but that requires installing their utility, and it's not as seamless as the hardware-level protection on the WD My Passport. If you're a photographer, videographer, or digital nomad who works in less-than-pristine conditions, the T7 Shield is a no-brainer. It's fast enough for active work, durable enough for real-world abuse, and priced reasonably for a 2 TB SSD.

Buying Guide: How to Choose the Best External Hard Drive

1. Capacity vs. Speed: Know Your Workflow
If you're archiving completed projects or backing up a desktop, an HDD like the WD My Passport gives you the most gigabytes per dollar. But if you edit video, run applications, or transfer files daily, an SSD's speed will save you hours every week. Don't buy a 5 TB HDD for active video editing—you'll hate the lag. Conversely, don't buy a 2 TB SSD if you just need to store old tax documents.

2. Portability and Durability
If the drive will live on your desk, a standard HDD is fine. If you travel, commute, or work in the field, prioritize SSDs with rubber bumpers or IP ratings. The Samsung T7 Shield is built for abuse; the WD My Passport is not. A drop from waist height can kill a spinning HDD instantly, while an SSD will usually survive.

3. Connection and Compatibility
All three drives use USB-C, but check your computer's ports. The SanDisk Extreme Pro needs USB 3.2 Gen 2x2 to hit its peak speed—most laptops only have Gen 2 (10 Gbps). If you have an older PC with USB-A, you'll need an adapter. For the fastest speeds, ensure your computer supports at least USB 3.2 Gen 2.

4. Security and Encryption
Hardware encryption (like on the WD My Passport) is automatic and doesn't slow down the drive. Software encryption (on the SSDs) works but requires you to install and manage a utility. If you store sensitive client data or personal documents, hardware encryption is worth the extra consideration.

5. Warranty and Longevity
SSDs have no moving parts and typically last longer than HDDs under normal use. The SanDisk Extreme Pro's 5-year warranty is the best here, reflecting confidence in its build. HDDs are more prone to mechanical failure over time, so consider a longer warranty a sign of reliability.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use an external SSD for gaming?
Yes, but with caveats. For loading games on a console, an SSD like the Samsung T7 Shield works well for older titles. For modern PC games, an internal NVMe drive is still faster. External SSDs are best for storing your game library rather than running demanding titles directly from them.

Is 5 TB of HDD storage safer than 2 TB of SSD storage?
Not really. SSDs are more resistant to physical shock and have no moving parts, making them less likely to fail from drops. HDDs offer more capacity but are fragile. For critical data, always keep a second backup—no single drive is completely safe.

Do I need to format a new external drive before using it?
Most come pre-formatted for Windows (NTFS) or Mac (exFAT). If you're using a Mac, the WD My Passport may need reformatting to exFAT for compatibility. The SSDs typically work out of the box with both systems. Check the manufacturer's instructions before use.

What's the difference between USB 3.2 Gen 2 and Gen 2x2?
USB 3.2 Gen 2 offers up to 10 Gbps (about 1,000 MB/s), while Gen 2x2 doubles that to 20 Gbps (2,000 MB/s). The SanDisk Extreme Pro supports Gen 2x2, but your computer must also support it to achieve those speeds. Most current laptops only support Gen 2.
